1 · The problem, in plain words

For a 15-year-old: Vikram started a company that makes software for trucking and delivery businesses. To grow, he needs money from investors called VCs (venture capitalists). He has a list of 50 of them. Writing to an investor is like asking someone to join your team: if you ask when they're already thinking about your topic (they just wrote about trucks, or just invested in a delivery company), they're far more likely to say yes. If you ask someone who has been silent for three months, you're probably wasting your time.

Vikram's list tells him who the investors are and when he last spoke to them. It doesn't tell him who is "warm" right now. Finding that out means searching the internet for 50 people, twice a week. Nobody has time for that, so he guesses.

Our job: build a robot that does that searching twice a week, reads everything, decides who is warm and why (with proof he can click), tells him who is quiet, and writes the answer into his list. The robot must never scribble over his own private notes.

What exactly has to happen (from the brief)

  1. Twice a week, before Vikram starts work, check all 50 investors.
  2. Use observable signals from the last 30 days: something they wrote, said in an interview, invested in, or appeared at.
  3. Say who is worth contacting this week, and why. Not a hunch.
  4. Also say who is quiet, so he knows where not to spend energy.
  5. Update his table each run, so it is already up to date when he opens it.
  6. Update only 4 columns (last signal date, signal summary, follow-up score, recommended action). His contact history and notes must stay untouched.
  7. Exam rules: live external data; working with data at submission; Gemini Flash only (no Claude API); deployed on Vercel; submit URL + GitHub + Components Map.

B. No-code automation (Zapier/Make)RSS → AI step → Google Sheet rowFast to start, but row-update steps typically rewrite whole rows, which risks overwriting notes (the critical rule). Hard to enforce "only 4 columns", to test, to explain the score, or to add guardrails. Paid per task.
C. Scrape LinkedIn / XPull each VC's posts directlyRichest signal, but against their terms; accounts get banned; brittle (pages change). Ethically and legally weak for a graded project.
D. Paid data (Crunchbase / PitchBook APIs)Structured deals and people dataExpensive, needs approval; covers deals but not "wrote / said / appeared"; not "live" in the way news is.
E. "Ask the AI" only (Gemini + Google Search for each VC)One prompt per VC: "what did they do in 30 days?"Simplest, but (1) Google Search grounding isn't in the free Gemini plan, (2) the AI may invent dates or links (hallucination), (3) scores would change run to run and be unexplainable. ✔ G. One-line answer for the viva: "AI is good at reading and judging meaning, bad at being consistent and honest about facts. So we let Gemini do only the judging, on real evidence we fetched, and let code and the database handle facts, maths and safety."

3 · The Nine Checks

Kill switches (01-03): if any fails, don't build. Sizing (04, 05, 08): is it worth it? Boundary (06, 07, 09): where must humans stay in charge? The Cut is what we deliberately left to Vikram.

4 · Components Map and how one run works

  1. Trigger: Vercel Cron (Mon + Thu 01:30 UTC = 07:00 IST) or the Refresh button. Only one run at a time (a unique index in the database); the scheduled run always wins over a half-finished manual one.
  2. Input: the 50 rows of Vikram's table in Neon.
  3. Context (live): for each investor, in parallel: Google News for the person; Google News for the firm + logistics words (deals); the blog/Substack feed on file; Apple Podcasts. Only items with a real http(s) link and a source date in the last 30 days. Each source keeps its own share, so a strong older deal isn't crowded out.
  4. AI (Gemini Flash): 10 investors per call, strict JSON schema, temperature 0.1. For each item: actor (person / firm / not them), kind, logistics fit 0-3, event id, summary; per investor: overall summary and email hook (with the item it's based on).
  5. Code checks the AI: the text must name the investor (or, for a deal, name the firm or be explicitly about logistics); "3/3 logistics" needs a logistics word in the text; blog posts count for the partner only if they're the author; one real-world event counts once; the hook must come from a kept item.
  6. Rules (code): score and action from fixed weights plus Vikram's status and last-contact date (read-only). For quiet investors: a 1-year lookback of news naming them with their firm.
  7. Output: one targeted UPDATE of the 4 signal columns per investor through the restricted pulse_engine login. Evidence, score changes and the run log go to separate tables. A fingerprint of Vikram's columns is taken before and after.
  8. Human gate: Vikram reviews, clicks the evidence, asks Gemini for a draft, edits and sends it himself, and updates his own columns.

5 · How do we know it works? (accuracy, tests, runs)

100%
precision: no false claims about who did what (57 hand-labelled items)
89%
recall: found 24 of 27 real signals; misses were headlines that didn't name the investor
48/48
automated tests pass (22 scoring rules + 26 database permissions)
0
errors in the final live runs; notes fingerprint identical before and after every run

How the accuracy test works: we took 57 real items from the live pipeline and labelled each by hand (is it really about this investor or their firm? is it about logistics?). Then we ran them through Gemini plus our code checks (tests/eval_judge.py) and compared. The topic label was 100% right on the correct items. This was measured on the fallback model (Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite), so it's the worst case.

Why precision over recall: a missed signal costs Vikram a little; a wrong one ("congrats on your post" when a colleague wrote it) could embarrass him in front of an investor. So the rules are strict on purpose.

Live runs: all 50 investors in 40-60 seconds; ~400 public items checked per run; ~60-80 kept as real signals; both the Refresh path and the scheduled cron path tested on the real server.

8 · Words you need to know

APIA way for programs to ask other programs for things over the internet (like a waiter taking your order to the kitchen).
API key / secretA password for an API. Ours live in Vercel's hidden settings, never in GitHub.
Serverless functionCode that wakes up when called; we don't manage any computer.
CronA schedule for servers. "30 1 * * 1,4" = 01:30 UTC every Monday and Thursday.
RSSA public list of a site's newest articles, made for apps.
LLMLarge language model: AI that reads and writes language (Gemini).
Prompt / system instructionThe AI's job description and rules.
Structured output (JSON schema)Forcing the AI to answer in an exact format so code can read it.
TemperatureCreativity dial: 0.1 for judging (consistent), 0.6 for email drafts.
HallucinationAI stating something false. Prevented: the AI only labels real items with real links.
GroundingMaking AI answer from real sources (our fetched items).
Precision / recallPrecision: of what it flagged, how much was right. Recall: of what was really there, how much it found.
Least privilegeEach part gets only the permissions it needs.
TransactionSeveral database changes that all succeed or all fail.
Hash / fingerprintA short code computed from data; any change changes it. Proves notes didn't change.
HMAC tokenA tamper-proof ticket made with a secret; proves you started a run.
CSPContent-Security-Policy: browser rule that only our own scripts may run.
Rate limit / quotaHow many requests a service allows (free Gemini 3.5 Flash: 20 per day).
FallbackPlan B: if one model is busy, use the next.
DeterministicSame input → same output (our scoring code).
Prompt injectionText inside data trying to give the AI orders. Limited here: the AI can only fill a fixed schema, and code checks its answers.

9 · Problems we hit while building (good viva stories)

  • Gemini Google Search = "quota exceeded". Not in the free plan → built on free official feeds; search switches on automatically if billing is enabled.
  • 3.5 Flash took 80 s for 5 investors. "Thinking level: low" → 25 s with the same quality.
  • 20 calls/day limit. Batched 10 investors per call; skip exhausted models; Lite for drafts.
  • Vercel couldn't find helper files. Added the folder to Python's path in every endpoint (test on the real server!).
  • Accuracy problems found by auditing real outputs (wrong firm, car reviews, colleague's posts): fixed with code checks, then measured (100% precision).

11 · Viva questions and answers

1. The problem in one sentence?

Tell a fundraising founder which of his 50 investors are worth contacting this week, with clickable proof from the last 30 days, and keep his pipeline table updated twice a week without ever touching his own notes.

2. Where exactly is the AI, and why is it needed?

Gemini Flash reads each headline and decides whether it's really this investor, what they did, and whether it's about logistics, then writes the summary, hook and drafts. Keyword rules can't tell "Peak XV led a round" from "a company Peak XV once backed raised from others".

3. Why not let the AI decide the score?

Scores must be consistent and explainable. AI judges meaning; code does the arithmetic. Same evidence → same score, shown line by line.

4. How do you prevent hallucinations?

The AI only labels items we fetched (real URL + source date); strict JSON; temperature 0.1; code checks (evidence names them, logistics word, author check, one event once, hook from a kept item); rejected items stay visible.

5. How accurate is it?

On 57 hand-labelled live items: 100% precision, 89% recall, 95% overall; topic label 100%. Measured on the fallback Lite model (worst case).

6. Why prefer precision over recall?

A missed signal costs little; a false "congrats on your post" damages trust with an investor.

7. Prove the notes can't be overwritten.

Column-level Postgres permissions: pulse_engine can UPDATE only the 4 signal columns. Live "attack" shows "permission denied"; per-run MD5 fingerprints match; updates target 4 columns by id, never whole rows.

8. Why two database logins?

Separation of duties: the robot can't touch notes; the edit screen can't fake a score. The owner login isn't on the server at all.

9. What is the live external data?

Google News RSS (person; firm + logistics), the investors' blog/Substack RSS, the Apple Podcasts API, all fetched at run time.

10. Why not LinkedIn / X?

Scraping breaks their terms and gets accounts banned. A real product would use official APIs with permission.

11. Why Neon, not Supabase?

Both are Postgres; schema and permissions are plain SQL and run unchanged on Supabase; the free Supabase slots are used by earlier projects. Switching = 2 connection strings.

12. How is "twice a week" implemented?

Vercel Cron calls /api/cron with a secret at 01:30 UTC Mon/Thu. It processes 50 investors in parallel within a 200-second budget.

13. What if Gemini or a news source is down?

Gemini falls back across three Flash models. If the AI or a news source still fails for an investor, they keep last run's verdict and the run is marked "partial". We never wrongly call someone "quiet".

14. How do you know someone is quiet for 3 months, not just 30 days?

For investors with no 30-day signal, a 1-year news search for name + firm together (avoids namesakes) gives the last mention date.

15. How does the relationship affect the advice?

Followed up under 14 days ago → WAIT (hook saved). Waiting 14+ days + fresh signal → NUDGE. Waiting 45+ days and quiet → "one light check-in next month, or move on".

16. Same person, different name problem?

Evidence must name the investor or firm; lookback uses name + firm; podcasts need the exact full name; Gemini's actor check rejects namesakes (e.g. a different Neeraj Arora).

17. What stops two runs colliding?

A unique index allows only one "running" run; the scheduled run supersedes a stale manual run; batch calls need an HMAC run ticket.

18. What does a run cost?

About ₹0: ~5 Gemini calls (free tier), free feeds, Vercel Hobby, Neon free.

19. How did you choose the weights?

Expert heuristics from the brief: a deal in your space is the strongest signal, then writing, speaking, events; freshness decays over 30 days; timing respects follow-up etiquette. They're in one place in code and the next step is learning them from which emails got replies.

20. What does the JSON schema do?

It forces Gemini to return exact fields with allowed values (e.g. kind ∈ invested/wrote/said/appeared/mentioned/other), so code can rely on it.

21. Why Gemini 3.5 Flash with "thinking: low"?

Careful enough for judging, 3× faster than default thinking; Lite models as fallback.

22. Security?

Secrets only in Vercel env; least-privilege DB logins; HMAC run tickets; CSP + http(s)-only links; editor key in a header; rate limits on public AI actions.

23. Is anything fake?

Investors, firms, theses and every signal are real and live. Vikram, "Haulr" and his notes are illustrative because he is a case character.

24. Why not an AI agent?

The task is a fixed pipeline; an agent adds unpredictability and would need write access near the notes.

25. How did you test?

22 scoring-rule tests (offline), 26 live permission tests, a 57-item accuracy evaluation, end-to-end runs on both run paths, and a harsh audit with every finding fixed.

26. What is the human's role?

Vikram decides whom to contact, edits and sends every email, and owns his notes. The tool only recommends.

27. Limitations?

Headline-level evidence (no full-article reading); public activity only; event coverage limited without paid search; weights not yet learned from outcomes.

28. What would make it a product?

Login and per-founder data (row-level security), Gmail integration to log replies (with consent), Slack/Telegram brief, learning weights from replies, paid search grounding for events.

29. How does Vikram add investors?

An "Add investor" form or CSV import (via his private link), using the founder login, which can't touch signal columns.

30. What did you learn?

Use AI for judgement on real evidence, box it in with a schema and context, verify it with code, measure it, and let the database enforce the rules that matter most.
